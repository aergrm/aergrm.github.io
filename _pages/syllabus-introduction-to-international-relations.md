> International relations asks why states fight, why they cooperate, how markets and institutions shape political choices, and whose interests are protected or neglected in the global system. This course gives students a practical set of analytical tools for answering those questions with theory, evidence, and careful comparison.

## Course Description

The UWM catalog describes POL SCI 102 as a course focused on understanding and explaining the foreign policies of states, with particular attention to military security, economic relations, and consequences for the international system. The course carries Social and Behavioral Science general education credit.

This course introduces students to the central concepts, theories, and empirical questions of international relations. We examine how interests, strategic interaction, domestic politics, institutions, identities, and unequal power shape outcomes in world politics. The course begins with foundational approaches to international relations and then applies them to war, coercion, terrorism, international organizations, trade, finance, development, human rights, migration, climate change, global health, and emerging technologies.

Rather than treating current events as isolated stories, students will learn to use analytical frameworks to explain why events unfold as they do, compare alternative explanations, evaluate evidence, and communicate defensible conclusions.

### Central Questions

- Why does international conflict occur even when war is costly?
- When do threats, deterrence, sanctions, diplomacy, and reassurance work?
- How do domestic institutions, leaders, public opinion, and bureaucracies shape foreign policy?
- When can international law and institutions facilitate cooperation?
- How do trade, finance, development, and inequality distribute gains and losses across societies?
- How are climate change, migration, global health, cyber conflict, artificial intelligence, and nuclear risk transforming international politics?

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of the course, students should be able to:

1. **Explain** major approaches to international relations, including realism, liberalism, constructivism, bargaining theory, and domestic-politics approaches.
2. **Identify** key state and nonstate actors and describe how power, interests, ideas, and institutions affect their behavior.
3. **Apply** theories of conflict and cooperation to historical and contemporary cases.
4. **Interpret** basic maps, tables, graphs, and cross-national evidence used in the study of world politics.
5. **Compare** competing explanations and assess the strengths and limitations of the evidence supporting them.
6. **Construct** a clear argument in writing and support it with credible sources and appropriate citations.
7. **Participate** in structured negotiation and reflect on the strategic, ethical, and distributional consequences of policy choices.

## Course Materials

### Required Text

Frieden, Jeffry A., David A. Lake, Kenneth A. Schultz, Susan D. Hyde, and Stephanie J. Rickard. 2026. *World Politics: Interests, Interactions, Institutions*. 6th ed. New York: W. W. Norton.

Students may use a print, rental, or electronic copy. Additional readings, primary documents, data visualizations, podcasts, and short videos will be available through Canvas or the UWM Libraries. Students will not be required to purchase access to news sites.

### Reading Strategy

Students should complete readings before class and be prepared to answer four questions:

1. What is the author's central claim?
2. What mechanism connects the cause to the outcome?
3. What evidence supports the claim?
4. What alternative explanation or limitation should we consider?

## Course Design and Expectations

Class meetings combine concise lectures, guided discussion, case analysis, data interpretation, short writing exercises, and simulations. Students learn international relations most effectively when they practice using concepts rather than only hearing definitions.

UWM's credit-hour policy expects at least 48 hours of student work per credit. A three-credit course therefore represents approximately 144 total hours during the semester. A typical week will include about 2.5 hours in class and 6–7 hours for reading, preparation, assignments, and study.

## Assessment and Grading

| Component | Weight |
|---|---:|
| Preparation quizzes | 10% |
| Engagement and contribution | 10% |
| Data and map labs | 10% |
| Analytical memo | 15% |
| Midterm examination | 15% |
| Crisis bargaining simulation and debrief | 15% |
| Final policy brief | 25% |
| **Total** | **100%** |

### Preparation Quizzes — 10%

Short Canvas quizzes will check comprehension of core concepts and help students prepare for class. The lowest two quiz scores will be dropped. Quizzes are open-note but must be completed independently.

### Engagement and Contribution — 10%

Engagement includes informed participation in discussion, small-group work, brief in-class writing, peer feedback, and constructive contributions to simulations. Students who are less comfortable speaking in a large group may demonstrate engagement through small-group reporting, written exit tickets, or designated Canvas discussions. Quality and preparation matter more than frequency.

### Data and Map Labs — 10%

Four short labs will ask students to interpret evidence such as conflict trends, alliance networks, trade flows, sanctions data, migration patterns, or public-opinion results. No prior statistical training is required. Students will explain what the evidence shows, what it does not show, and how it relates to a theoretical claim.

### Analytical Memo — 15%

Students will write a 1,000–1,200 word memo applying two competing theoretical approaches to a common case. The memo must identify each approach's assumptions and mechanisms, use evidence from assigned materials, and explain which account is more persuasive. Students will receive a prompt, model paragraph, rubric, and an opportunity for peer review.

### Midterm Examination — 15%

The midterm will include concept identification, short applications, and one comparative essay. A study guide and sample questions will be provided. The examination is closed-internet; students may bring one handwritten double-sided note sheet.

### Crisis Bargaining Simulation and Debrief — 15%

Students will represent governments or international actors in a multilateral crisis. Teams will receive public background materials and confidential role instructions. Assessment is based on preparation, strategic reasoning, evidence-based participation, and a 750-word individual debrief. Students are not graded on whether their side “wins.”

### Final Policy Brief — 25%

The final assignment is a 1,500–1,800 word policy brief addressing a contemporary international problem. Students will define the problem, identify relevant actors and interests, apply at least two course concepts, evaluate policy options, make a recommendation, and acknowledge risks and tradeoffs. The project is scaffolded through a question proposal, source audit, thesis workshop, draft exchange, and final reflection.

## Course Policies

### Attendance and Absences

Regular attendance is important because many learning activities cannot be reproduced by reading slides. Students may miss two class meetings without documentation or penalty to the engagement grade. Beyond those absences, students should contact me as soon as reasonably possible. Absences related to documented disability accommodations, religious observance, military service, university-sponsored activities, illness, caregiving emergencies, or other serious circumstances will be handled in accordance with university policy.

Students remain responsible for checking Canvas, obtaining notes, and completing work after an absence. I will help students identify a reasonable path forward, but I do not provide a second private lecture for each missed class.

### Late Work and Flexibility

Preparation quizzes generally cannot be made up because two scores are dropped. Each student has one automatic 48-hour extension that may be used on the analytical memo, simulation debrief, or final policy brief by notifying me before the original deadline. Longer extensions may be granted for serious or documented circumstances. Late work without an extension normally loses 5 percentage points per calendar day, up to five days.

### Communication

Course announcements and materials will be posted on Canvas. I will normally respond to email within two business days. Messages should include the course number and a clear subject line. Questions that require discussion are often handled more effectively during office hours or an appointment.

### Discussion and Classroom Climate

International politics raises contested moral and political questions. Disagreement is expected; personal attacks are not. Students should criticize arguments rather than people, support claims with evidence, listen before responding, and avoid treating any student as a spokesperson for a country, religion, ethnicity, ideology, or identity. We will distinguish analysis of an argument from endorsement of that argument.

Some course materials address war, terrorism, displacement, repression, and mass violence. I will provide advance notice when material is especially graphic and will offer a reasonable alternative when the learning objective can be met without exposure to graphic content.

### Technology

Laptops and tablets may be used for course-related work. During simulations, discussions, peer review, and designated activities, students may be asked to close devices unless an accommodation requires their use. Recording class meetings requires prior permission, except when authorized through an accommodation.

### Generative Artificial Intelligence

Generative AI tools may be used only when an assignment explicitly permits them. Permitted uses may include brainstorming search terms, testing an outline, or receiving feedback on clarity. Students may not use AI to complete quizzes or exams, fabricate sources, replace required reading, or generate text submitted as their own analysis.

When AI use is permitted, students must include a brief disclosure identifying the tool, the purpose for which it was used, and how the output was checked or revised. Students remain responsible for every claim, citation, and sentence they submit. Undisclosed or prohibited use may be addressed through UWM's academic misconduct procedures.

### Academic Integrity and Citation

Students must distinguish their ideas from the words, data, and arguments of others. All sources, including course materials, websites, datasets, and AI-generated content when permitted, must be acknowledged in the format specified for the assignment. Collaboration is encouraged only when an assignment authorizes it. Suspected academic misconduct will be handled under Wisconsin Administrative Code Chapter UWS 14 and UWM procedures.

### Incomplete Grades

An incomplete may be considered only when a student has completed a substantial portion of the course successfully but cannot finish a limited amount of work because of an unusual, substantiated circumstance beyond the student's control. Incompletes are not substitutes for a failing grade or for repeated missed work.

## Instructor Commitment

My responsibility is to make the course intellectually serious, transparent, and accessible. Students can expect clear learning objectives, assignment instructions and rubrics, timely feedback, consistent application of course policies, opportunities to ask questions, and respectful treatment. In return, I ask students to prepare, engage honestly, communicate early when problems arise, and take responsibility for their learning and for the quality of our shared classroom environment.

> **The syllabus is a roadmap, not a trap.** Policies establish common expectations, but they do not eliminate judgment or compassion. When circumstances interfere with learning, communicate early so that we can identify a fair and academically responsible solution.
