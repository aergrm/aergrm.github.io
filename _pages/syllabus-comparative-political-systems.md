## Course Description

How do political institutions shape democratic governance? Why do some countries have two dominant parties while others have many? What makes some democracies more stable than others? This course provides a systematic introduction to comparative political institutions, examining how different countries organize power, make decisions, and represent citizens.

We analyze political systems through a comparative lens, focusing on institutional design and its consequences. Rather than memorizing facts about individual countries, students learn to think critically about how institutions work, why they vary, and what effects they produce. Topics include electoral systems, party systems, executive–legislative relations, federalism, judicial review, central banks, and contemporary challenges to democracy.

The course emphasizes analytical thinking and comparative reasoning. Students develop the ability to identify patterns across political systems, evaluate institutional trade-offs, and construct evidence-based arguments about political outcomes.

## Prerequisites

There is no official prerequisite course except junior standing. Familiarity with material normally covered in POL SCI 103, *Introduction to Political Science*, or POL SCI 104, *Introduction to American Government and Politics*, is helpful.

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of the course, successful students will be able to:

1. Analyze how key political institutions—electoral systems, party systems, legislatures, executives, and courts—function across democratic systems.
2. Compare and contrast the Westminster (majoritarian) and consensus models of democracy, evaluating their strengths, weaknesses, and appropriate contexts.
3. Explain causal relationships between institutional design and political outcomes, including how electoral rules affect party systems, federal structures affect economic coordination, and executive–legislative relations shape foreign-policy decisions.
4. Apply comparative frameworks to real-world political events and policy choices, including economic governance and foreign-policy decisions.
5. Evaluate institutional trade-offs and reform proposals using evidence and theoretical reasoning.
6. Assess how domestic political institutions constrain and enable economic and foreign-policy choices in democratic systems.
7. Synthesize information from multiple sources to produce an original, evidence-based analysis of a country's political system.
8. Communicate complex political analysis clearly and persuasively in writing.

## Course Structure and Time Commitment

This is a three-credit course over a fifteen-week semester. Students should plan to dedicate approximately eight to ten hours each week:

- Class attendance: approximately 3 hours.
- Reading and studying: approximately 4–5 hours.
- Assignments, essays, and the country project: approximately 2–3 hours, varying by week.

The course meets twice weekly for lectures, discussions, and activities. Tuesdays typically focus on core concepts and theory; Thursdays emphasize application, discussion, and student presentations. Lecture slides, study guides, readings, assignments, updates, and announcements are posted on Canvas.

### Attendance and Participation — 15%

This is an in-person course, and attendance is mandatory. Learning depends on active, consistent engagement with discussions and in-class activities. Students are expected to attend every session and arrive on time.

Students who must miss class should notify the instructor in advance whenever possible. Absences do not remove responsibility for obtaining notes, completing readings, and submitting required work. In-class work generally cannot be made up unless an absence is excused because of exceptional circumstances such as illness or a family emergency.

### Country Presentations — 10%

These presentations apply course concepts to real institutional cases, develop comparative reasoning, and allow the class to examine particular countries in depth.

Students present once in a group of three or four. Presentations are ten to twelve minutes and take place on Thursdays. Slides must include:

1. One institutional feature using Lijphart or other course concepts.
2. One mechanism explaining how that feature affects outcomes.
3. One explicit comparative contrast—“compared to what?”
4. One discussion question for the class.

Four in-class quizzes assess understanding of readings and core concepts. Each quiz is worth five percent of the final grade, contains approximately fifteen to twenty multiple-choice, true-or-false, and short-answer questions, and is open-notes but closed-book.

### In-Class Essays — 20%

Students complete two fifty-minute handwritten essays, each worth ten percent. A strong essay states a clear thesis in the first paragraph, explains a causal mechanism linking an institution to an outcome, makes an explicit comparison, uses concrete evidence, and briefly addresses a counterargument.

| Essay | Date | Topics |
|---|---|---|
| Essay 1 | Mar. 10 | Westminster and consensus models, electoral systems, and party systems |
| Essay 2 | Apr. 21 | Foreign-policy institutions and economic governance |

### Midterm Examination — 15%

The in-class midterm takes place Thursday, March 19. It contains short-answer and essay questions and covers material from Weeks 1–7. A study guide is provided one week before the examination.

### Country Analysis Project — 20%

Students conduct an in-depth analysis of one country's political system using Lijphart's framework. The project requires students to collect institutional data, locate the country on the conceptual map, and evaluate how effectively its democratic institutions serve its people.

The final paper is seven to eight double-spaced pages. Students analyze their selected country's institutions using Lijphart's ten characteristics, use Lijphart as the primary source, incorporate four to six additional scholarly sources, and present their analysis. Two interim submissions allow students to receive feedback on work in progress.

## Course Policies

### Late Work

Assignments are due on the assigned date. Late work is not normally accepted. Students facing an emergency should communicate as early as possible; documentation may be required, and extensions are considered case by case.

### Academic Integrity

Academic honesty is fundamental to the university community. Cheating, fabrication, plagiarism, facilitating academic dishonesty, unauthorized collaboration, submitting another person's work, failing to cite ideas or research, recycling work without permission, and sharing or receiving quiz or examination answers are prohibited under UWS Chapter 14.

Academic misconduct may result in a zero on the assignment, failure of the course, and referral to the Dean of Students Office.

### Generative Artificial Intelligence

The use of generative artificial intelligence is not allowed in this course. Using generative AI to assist with or complete an assignment is treated as academic misconduct under UWS Chapter 14. Students uncertain whether an online learning-support platform or generative AI tool is appropriate should consult the instructor before submitting work. All external sources must be properly cited.

### Communication

The instructor aims to respond to email within twenty-four hours on weekdays and forty-eight hours on weekends. Students should use their UWM email account, include “POL SCI 335” in the subject line, use professional language and tone, and consult the syllabus and Canvas before emailing.

### Religious Observance, Military Duty, and Incompletes

Reasonable accommodation is provided for religious observances and active military duty. Students should communicate conflicts as early as possible. A grade of Incomplete is reserved for exceptional circumstances in which a student who has completed most of the coursework cannot finish because of circumstances beyond the student's control.

### Accessibility and Accommodations

Students who need accommodations should register with UWM's Accessibility Resource Center and contact the instructor as early as possible, ideally within the first two weeks. All accommodations are handled confidentially.

### Student Support Resources

Students may seek assistance from the UWM Writing Center, UWM Libraries, tutoring services, the Norris Health Center, crisis services, and the Dean of Students Office.
